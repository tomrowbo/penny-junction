# Teach Glasses Once, Automate Physical Work

The strongest Meta entry is **“Show Me Once,” a packing-station agent that learns a short physical workflow from one narrated first-person demonstration, supervises the next attempt, catches a deliberate mistake, and completes the digital work only when the physical work is correct**. It is a better interpretation of the referenced AI-ATL project than copying its face-recognition use case: retain the immediate wearable loop, but replace personal-data lookup with temporal task memory and a consequential, verifiable action. The winning demo is compact: an expert packs once, the agent extracts four or five steps, a novice inserts the wrong SKU, the glasses intervene, and correction causes a label to print and inventory to decrement. That sequence proves a wearable-specific benefit—hands remain occupied while the system sees the same work the wearer sees—and scores across Meta’s four equally weighted categories: creativity, technical execution, real-world feasibility, and accessible/trustworthy UX. ([Junction Meta challenge](https://hackjunction.app/participate/45/challenges/342)) Continuous video processing is not itself the goal or even a stated requirement; event-driven snapshots plus audio are safer, cheaper and easier to make reliable. The product thesis should be **“turn one expert demonstration into an executable workflow,”** not “an AI assistant that watches everything.”

## InstaRizz supplies the interaction grammar, not learning

The AI-ATL repository presents **InstaRizz** as a Ray-Ban social copilot: first-person video reaches Instagram Live, OpenCV identifies a person, a search workflow enriches that identity, and GPT-4 produces a short bio and conversation openers. That story has a compelling wearable rhythm—look, recognise, enrich, respond—but the checked-in implementation is substantially narrower. `main.py` captures the computer screen every three seconds, detects a face, compares it with a tiny local set of enrolled identities, calls a Magic Loops endpoint and prints the result; it does not connect directly to glasses or Instagram. ([README](https://github.com/coderkai03/AI-ATL-2024#instarizz); [`main.py`](https://github.com/coderkai03/AI-ATL-2024/blob/main/main.py)) The separate Streamlit app asks the user to type a name manually and renders the returned profile, rather than consuming the recognition pipeline. ([`app.py`](https://github.com/coderkai03/AI-ATL-2024/blob/main/app.py))

Most importantly, **the repository does not learn online, retain durable behavioural memory, or take a consequential external action**. Its known-face encodings load at startup, its checked-in identity set contains only two named folders, and its output ends as text or a printed payload. ([Known faces](https://github.com/coderkai03/AI-ATL-2024/tree/main/known_faces); [`main.py`](https://github.com/coderkai03/AI-ATL-2024/blob/main/main.py)) That is useful competitive guidance. A Junction entry can preserve the magical “one glance changes something” moment while making the missing stages visible: observe, recognise the current workflow state, remember an expert-taught rule, decide within a bounded policy, act through a real tool, verify the outcome, and update behaviour after a correction. Avoiding facial identification also removes the reference project’s largest trust liability without sacrificing its immediacy.

## Five concepts make the physical world actionable

Meta’s brief rewards a focused everyday problem where first-person view produces an interaction better than phone mirroring, backed by a prototype that handles permissions, errors, connectivity and device-state changes. It gives equal consideration to display and audio-first experiences and to web or Device Access Toolkit integrations. ([Junction Meta challenge](https://hackjunction.app/participate/45/challenges/342)) Meanwhile, Meta’s own developer showcase already includes plant analysis, object finding, drawing guidance, study tracking and live visual assistance, so another system that merely identifies or advises will look incremental. ([Meta developer showcase](https://developers.meta.com/blog/explore-whats-possible-with-wearables-device-access-toolkit/)) The differentiator is downstream actuation.

| Rank | Concept | What it learns | Meaningful action | Why it can win |
|---:|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Show Me Once packing station** | Item sequence, packing material, exception rules and completion cue | Blocks a bad pack; then prints label and decrements inventory | Objective ground truth, unmistakable error/recovery, highly reliable tabletop demo |
| **2** | **Field-service apprentice** | Tool/part sequence, completion evidence and expert exceptions | Closes a work order with evidence and drafts a missing-part request | Best story about capturing tacit knowledge; slightly harder visual recognition |
| **3** | **Event-room reset autopilot** | Correct room layout, required objects and AV state | Creates missing-item tasks, controls lights/display and marks room ready | Uses the venue itself and produces a strong physical finale |
| **4** | **Venue-specific waste station** | Locally demonstrated exceptions to generic recycling rules | Unlocks or lights the correct bin and creates a contamination task | Inclusive, everyday and memorable, but risks seeming like simple classification |
| **5** | **Golden-shelf restock loop** | Desired arrangement and facings from an initial walkthrough | Creates a restock ticket and updates inventory | Commercially obvious and easy to stage, but less original |

Several other ideas remain viable but weaker for this weekend. A lab chain-of-custody assistant is technically impressive but creates avoidable safety and credibility questions. A personalised gesture-to-action layer could score exceptionally well on accessibility, yet reliable custom gesture learning is harder to prove quickly. “Where did I put it?” episodic memory is intuitive but its final action is less spectacular. The packing workflow is the best risk-adjusted choice because every state has visible ground truth, the second run can differ from the demonstration, and a printer or green light gives judges a physical proof that the agent acted.

## A 75-second demo proves learning and action

The demonstration should begin with an explicit voice command: **“Learn this packing workflow.”** The capture indicator becomes visible, and an expert packs an order while narrating only two semantic rules, such as “fragile products go in last” and “this sticker means cold chain.” A companion screen turns the stream into a four- or five-step timeline, then asks the expert to confirm the inferred rules before activation. This first phase is crucial: it makes the learning legible instead of allowing judges to assume that the flow was hardcoded.

The novice then starts a different but analogous order. The system recognises the order, observes the sequence and maintains a compact state such as `expected items`, `observed items`, `current step`, `active rules` and `confidence`. The novice deliberately adds the wrong product or closes the box before the fragile item. The glasses respond with a short audio or display intervention, the dashboard highlights the evidence frame, and the label action remains visibly locked. After the novice corrects the box, the system verifies the state, prints a real or simulated label, decrements mock inventory and marks the order complete. The final screen should connect cause to effect: **“Rule learned from demonstration → violation detected → correction verified → label printed.”**

One non-happy path should be part of the rehearsed story rather than hidden in the deck. Disconnect the glasses or introduce an unknown object; the system should pause, say that confidence is insufficient, and offer a QR/manual fallback before resuming. This directly answers Meta’s technical-execution requirement around connectivity, errors, permissions and device state. ([Junction Meta challenge](https://hackjunction.app/participate/45/challenges/342)) The pitch can then show a plausible pilot: one packing bench, five workflows and measurable mis-pack or training-time reduction before wider rollout.

## A bounded state machine keeps the build credible

The weekend architecture should be deliberately hybrid. Meta’s Device Access Toolkit provides camera, audio and motion access, while its camera sample supports live feed, still capture and recording with optional glasses-microphone audio. ([Meta Wearables FAQ](https://developers.meta.com/wearables/faq/); [CameraAccess sample](https://github.com/meta/meta-wearables-dat-ios/blob/main/samples/CameraAccess/README.md)) The glasses or phone should send sampled frames and speech events to a perception service. A multimodal model converts those observations into a constrained event object—`object`, `action`, `workflow step`, `confidence` and `evidence frame`—rather than controlling tools directly. A deterministic workflow engine owns legal state transitions and decides whether the next action is permitted. The action adapter then calls a mock inventory endpoint and either a label-printer webhook or a local print simulator; the verification stage checks the response and records it in an auditable timeline.

This design turns “learning” into something testable. The expert demonstration produces a small structured workflow and one or two conditional rules, which the user confirms. A later correction updates that rule store and visibly changes the next run. It is not model training, and the pitch should not pretend otherwise; it is **learning a task policy from demonstration and feedback**. That phrasing is both honest and technically defensible.

Scope should stop at **one workflow, five to ten visually distinct objects, four to six states and two downstream tools**. Use QR or AprilTag fallback for object identity if open-world visual recognition becomes unreliable. Event-driven capture is preferable to sending every frame because it reduces latency, bandwidth, battery drain and privacy exposure while preserving the live interaction. The official platform supports developer-bounded web actions through WebMCP, but an ordinary phone/server API is enough for the hackathon if WebMCP access becomes a dependency risk. ([Meta Wearables FAQ](https://developers.meta.com/wearables/faq/); [Official WebMCP samples](https://github.com/meta/meta_glasses_webmcp)) Rehearsal should work from a recorded feed or simulator, with live glasses enhancing rather than determining whether the demo survives.

## Trust becomes a visible product feature

Meta assigns **25% of judging to UX, input, accessibility and trust**, so consent cannot live only in speaker notes. ([Junction Meta challenge](https://hackjunction.app/participate/45/challenges/342)) Capture should start only after an explicit command, show a persistent indicator, stop when the named workflow finishes and offer “forget this session.” Store structured task events and a few approved evidence frames rather than continuous raw video. Avoid face identification, bystander profiling and background-speech retention; the booth demonstration should use consenting participants and controlled objects.

Actions need risk tiers. Reversible local events such as advancing a checklist or changing a light can happen automatically. Printing, creating a work item or sending an external message should require one clear confirmation. Purchases, locks, machinery and safety-critical operations should remain outside the autonomous demo. When confidence is low or observations conflict, the agent must ask rather than act. Every decision should expose the evidence frame, learned rule and tool result, with an undo control where applicable.

Accessibility should shape both output modes. A Display user can receive a high-contrast, glanceable instruction with colour plus icon/text rather than colour alone; an audio-first user receives the same state through concise speech and a confirmation gesture or voice command. Adjustable pacing, repetition and a phone companion view make the workflow usable across visual, hearing, speech and cognitive differences. The strongest trust line for judges is simple: **“It automates known states and asks on unknown ones.”**

## Conclusion

The reference project’s real lesson is not facial recognition. It is that first-person computing becomes compelling when perception collapses into one timely next step. “Show Me Once” extends that grammar into a complete agent loop whose learning, intervention, action and verification are all visible within a minute.

The most important strategic choice is to keep the intelligence narrow enough that the physical proof never fails. One confirmed expert rule applied to a different order is more convincing than a broad assistant that claims to understand an entire workplace. If the team can make a wrong object stop the workflow and a correction cause a real label to appear, it will have a memorable answer to every judging category—and a clear reason the product belongs on glasses rather than a phone.
